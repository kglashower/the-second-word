The Second Word: Responsive Leadership and the Missing Institution in Four Readings on Pastoral Formation

Kyle Glashower · DMIN 7023 — Spirit-Empowered Leadership · Southeastern University · Reading Critique, Module Two · October 30, 2026

The four books and their colors

  • Jeremy SimsThe Way of the Spirit: Recognizing and Responding to God's Subtle MovementsInterVarsity Press, 2026Attentiveness as gift; formation as slow trust.

Introduction: Peterson’s Triangle as Baseline

None of the four books I read was written specifically with an executive leader in mind. Peterson writes for the parish pastor, Barton for the exhausted minister on retreat, Sims for the soul in a season it cannot name, and Rolheiser for the adult who has already gotten life together and wonders why it still feels thin.

I read them from a desk at OneHope, where my responsibility is technology and operations for a global ministry, and from a Sunday role as a volunteer elder over our youth ministry. The books speak most naturally to the second role. The question I bring to them is whether they have anything to say to the first, and whether the gap is their limitation or mine.

Eugene Peterson supplies the frame. Pastoral work has three visible lines, preaching, teaching, and administration, and three hidden angles that give those lines their shape: prayer, Scripture, and spiritual direction. His charge is that American pastors have kept the lines and abandoned the angles, so that they have metamorphosed into a company of shopkeepers.1 The three angles share a single posture.

Prayer is never the first word; it is always the second word.2 Reading Scripture is not the same as listening to God, and must become so.3 Spiritual direction rests on the conviction that God is always doing something in a life before the pastor arrives.4 In every angle the pastor responds; God initiates.

Ronald Rolheiser, Jeremy Sims, and Ruth Haley Barton say the same thing in different registers, and that convergence is the subject of this paper. My thesis is that the four readings together give a true and necessary account of the leader’s interior life and an insufficient account of pastoral leadership. They teach the leader to recognize and respond to God’s prior action, and they are right to.

But none of them gives the leader a way to exercise the initiative that institutions require, direction, resources, measurement, and decision, without treating that initiative as the shopkeeper’s temptation. Because I’ve already written about Peterson in depth, I treat him here as the standard against which the other three are read, and I organize the critique by four themes the books share: response, depth, restraint, and fidelity.

God Acts First

The first theme is the one on which all four authors agree without qualification. For Peterson, the biblical pray-ers did not set out to understand the human situation but to be a part of what was going on with God, experiencing the world not as a problem to be solved but as a reality in which God is acting.5

Barton reaches the same conclusion through Elijah at Horeb: Elijah never asked for guidance; guidance simply came in the context of his willingness to be with God in utter openness and vulnerability.6 Even the desire that drives a leader toward God is, on her account, a response, because right in the very center of our desire for God is God’s desire for us.7

Sims makes the claim the hinge of his book. The invitation of the spiritual life is not to bring God near, but to awaken to the nearness that already is.8 Attentiveness, the capacity his whole book is about, can become just another technique we try to master, and he warns against that reading of his own work: We do not will ourselves into wisdom, or depth, or even meaningful prayer. These are not rewards for effort.9

Rolheiser states the theological ground beneath all of them. Love, understood properly, is never a reward for being good. Goodness, rather, is always a consequence of having been loved.10 Generativity, the mark of mature discipleship, depends on prayer, because without it the leader oscillates between inflation and depression.11

The convergence commits a leader to something specific. Every situation is already inhabited by God’s action, and the leader’s first move is to notice rather than to act. I want to say plainly that this is right, and that it is the correction a leader in my position most needs. The shopkeeper’s error is to treat the parish, or the organization, as a field of problems to be solved by the pastor’s initiative.

The four readings refuse that error at its root. The question the rest of this paper pursues is what the posture costs when the leader also has to decide.

Depth and the Engineered Absence of Silence

The second theme is a shared diagnosis, and it is where the critique first opens. All four authors locate the leader’s shallowness in structures rather than in character. Rolheiser’s version is the sharpest. Because we can all be reached all the time, there is no longer any structural need for silence, and the result is a particular kind of person: not bad, just busy; not immoral, just distracted; not lacking in soul, just preoccupied.12

Peterson locates the same pressure inside the congregation, which fills the pastor’s schedule with meetings so that there is time for neither solitude nor leisure to be before God.13 Barton describes the end state, a spiritual starvation so advanced that, like an anorexic, the leader is past the point of desiring real food.14 Sims names the moral cost: Inattention doesn’t just leave us neutral. It makes us numb.15

The remedies the authors offer are personal. Barton gives solitude a threefold architecture, a physical place, a time set apart, and a place in our soul that is set apart for God and God alone, the last being especially necessary for those in ministry whose inner life is constantly drawn on for public use.16 Her most practical and least pious advice is that perhaps the most spiritual thing we could do is get more rest.17

Sims commends the desert, where silence acts as an unflinching mirror that exposes the dependencies and illusions of control a leader has mistaken for sustenance.18 Peterson prescribes Sabbath, uncluttered time and space to distance ourselves from the frenzy of our own activities so we can see what God has been and is doing.19

Here is the difficulty. The cause is institutional and cultural, and the cure is a retreat. None of the four asks what an organization, a staff rhythm, or a church calendar would have to change so that silence is no longer structurally impossible. Barton’s retreat presupposes a leader who can leave, which a bivocational pastor or a single parent in ministry cannot assume.

Rolheiser’s observation that adults hide our sloth by working hard so as not to have to face the more challenging task of doing our inner work is the one line in the set that turns the diagnosis back on the leader rather than on the culture, and it is the most useful sentence in the four books for someone in my position.20

But it, too, prescribes inner work rather than institutional change. The single exception is Peterson’s second definition of Sabbath, which is not for the pastor’s sake at all but for the congregation’s: time to detach so that they have a chance to deal with God without our poking around and kibitzing, free from our guidance that always tends toward manipulation.21

That is a structural proposal, because it changes what the congregation receives and not only what the pastor feels. It is the only one in the set.

Restraint and the Problem of Initiative

The third theme is the center of the critique. Each author commends restraint as the characteristic discipline of the mature leader, and each is right about what restraint protects. Peterson’s claim is the most counterintuitive for anyone trained to solve problems: The suggestion to do something is nearly always inappropriate, because people in spiritual trouble are troubled over some disorder or dissatisfaction in being, not doing.22

His example is George Fox, who needed a pastor strong enough not to have done something to or for him, because that restraint would have provided space for the Holy Spirit to initiate the new life.23 Rolheiser’s version is Mary’s pondering, which in the Hebrew sense means to hold, carry, and transform tension so as not to give it back in kind, knowing that whatever energies we do not transform we will transmit.24

Its opposite is amazement, letting the crowd’s energy flow through us and become the basis for our actions.25 Sims, writing of the dark night, says that intentional partnership with the Spirit is not about fixing anything. It is about surrendering the need to fix. It is about fidelity over feelings.26 Barton finds the control instinct even in prayer, where reliance on our own words can be one facet of a need to control things, to set the agenda.27

Restraint protects people from the leader. Peterson says it without softening: pastoral guidance always tends toward manipulation.28 Rolheiser’s image of the elder as the place where the amazement, the gossip, the negativity, and the scapegoating stop is the best theory of non-anxious leadership in the set, and I have found no better description of what an executive is for in a season of organizational fear.29

Yet the books fail at the point where restraint meets responsibility, and they fail in three distinct ways. First, Peterson names administration as one of his three visible lines and then gives it almost no constructive treatment. His two remarks are that crisp administration is far more stimulating than waiting in uncertainty for vision, and that the pastor without facility in means buys games and gimmicks and programs without end under the illusion of being practical.30

Both are true, and neither tells a leader how to run anything by the angles. Peterson can tell me how not to be shaped by operations; he cannot tell me how to shape them. Second, Rolheiser gives no criteria for when the leader should act on the crowd’s energy rather than absorb it. Prophetic leadership sometimes conducts.

His own insistence that charity gives to the poor while justice corrects the structures that create poverty, and that Jesus asks us to do both, demands exactly the kind of structural action the pondering chapters never authorize.31 Third, Sims never marks where surrendering the need to fix stops being a posture for the dark night and starts being a dereliction of duty.

A youth pastor with a volunteer in crisis cannot only attend. Fidelity over feelings is a rule for the soul under God’s hiddenness, not a rule for the leader with a decision due by Friday, and Sims does not draw the line.32

The books make a resolution possible without stating it. Peterson’s ordination charge imagines the congregation telling its new pastor that there will be years when neither of them feels like believing anything, and asking in advance for a vow precisely because the congregation knows it will later send a delegation demanding something else. The vows lash the pastor to the mast of word and sacrament so that the siren voices cannot be answered.33

Read as a theory of leadership rather than of ordination, this is the answer to the initiative problem. Initiative is not forbidden. It is to be exercised through commitments made before the pressure arrives, which is what a mission statement and a board are for in an organization and what a vow is for in a parish.

The four readings supply the interior. The leader must supply the structure, and the books do not say so.

Fidelity and Community

The fourth theme is a second convergence, and it is where the books correct their own individualism. On fidelity the three voices are nearly interchangeable. Peterson’s congregation tells its pastor that in the years when no one feels like believing, it doesn’t matter. Do it.34 Rolheiser’s one nonnegotiable rule of prayer is to show up regardless of feeling, because our rituals of prayer can sustain our goodwill and our energy, not the reverse.35

Sims says of the dark night that you pray, not because it works, but because it keeps you close.36 A critic may fairly ask whether this is perseverance or grim endurance. The texts answer: maturity is synonymous with our level of gratitude, and fidelity is sustained by what it is fidelity to, which is why Rolheiser warns against the perennial temptation to attempt virtue by willpower alone.37

On community the convergence corrects Barton, whose solitude is always singular. Rolheiser insists that we become saints only with the support of others and that some things will kill you if you face them alone.38 Sims closes his book on the same note: grace by its nature, requires community, and long tenure in one place means you don’t get to skip the hard parts.39

But Sims places community in his final waypoint after nine chapters of interior work, and the order tells where his weight sits. Peterson is the one who turns solitude outward from the start, since his Sabbath exists so that the congregation can deal with God without the pastor.40

Synthesis: Necessary and Insufficient

The four readings describe the angles and not the lines. They teach a leader to respond to God’s prior action, to recover depth, to restrain the reflex to fix, and to stay. On every one of those points they are right, and on none of them do they say what a leader is to do with the authority an institution places in her hands. That is their integrity and their limit.

Two cautions keep the critique honest. The first is that the executive’s sophisticated reasons for initiative are exactly what the books are aimed at. Peterson warns that the greatest errors in the spiritual life are not committed by the novices but by the adepts, and Rolheiser’s sloth disguised as hard work is a description of a working week I recognize.41

A critique of the readings must not become an alibi for the shopkeeper. The second concerns mediation. The readings assume that formation requires unmediated presence, the director in the room, the leader on retreat, the community at the table. Formation does travel through media, or no one would ever have been converted by reading a book, and an organization that delivers Scripture through print, film, and now conversational software stands on that fact.

But some goods do not travel: shared suffering, rupture and repair, duration. The readings underestimate mediation, and the synthetic substitutes now on offer overestimate what mediation can carry. I hold the tension rather than resolve it, because my work requires me to.

Ministry Application

Two practices follow, each read through the newest pressure on a leader’s attention, which is the artificial intelligence tooling now in the leader’s own hand.

The five-minute gap. Rolheiser’s sloth that hides behind hard work used to have a natural limit. Real work needed a minimum block of time, so the small gaps between meetings stayed empty, and for most of my working days those gaps were the only unscheduled silence in them.

Delegating a task to a language model has almost no startup cost, and the model returns finished work asynchronously, so there is now always something to start and something to check, and no interval stays empty.42 Barton’s first discovery in solitude was exhaustion she had been fighting through rather than admitting.43 My own first discovery was that I had eliminated the last structurally unproductive intervals in the day and called it diligence.

Rolheiser’s test is what the work lets me avoid. The updated form is narrower: notice what I reach for in the gaps, and whether any gap is still allowed to stay empty.

Blessing a displaced team. Rolheiser defines blessing as seeing a person, speaking well of them, and giving away some of your life so that they may have more, and cursing as using others’ lives to build up our own.44 A senior engineer on my team grieved for the better part of a month this year when the craft he loved was absorbed by a model, and he came out the other side building more than he ever had.

What he needed during the month was not a performance conversation. A leader who can only measure output cannot bless someone whose output is no longer the point, and Peterson’s reminder that Scripture knows of no successful churches is, read this way, a statement about where worth is located rather than a prohibition on counting.45

The two practices are one discipline facing in two directions. The first asks whether I will let any part of my own day stay empty enough for God to have the first word; the second asks whether I can see a person apart from what he produces. Both are harder than they were, because the same tool that fills my gaps also makes output the easiest thing about anyone to measure.

The readings name the discipline and leave the structure to me, and that is where their insufficiency becomes my responsibility: the empty interval and the unmeasured blessing will exist in my week and on my team only if I build them in on purpose.

Conclusion

I began by asking whether four books written for pastors have anything to say to an executive, and whether the gap between them and my work is their limitation or mine. The four themes have answered both halves. On response, depth, restraint, and fidelity the books agree with one another and are right, and in each case the thing they are right about is the thing my role makes easiest to abandon; that part of the gap is mine.

But the critique also held. At every point where the interior disciplines meet institutional responsibility, the books fall silent: Peterson names administration and leaves it, Rolheiser never says when to act rather than absorb, Sims never marks where surrender becomes neglect, and Barton’s solitude has no corporate form. That part of the gap is theirs, and it is a real one.

The readings are therefore necessary and insufficient in exactly the sense the thesis claimed. They form the leader who must then build what they do not describe, and the application section showed what that building looks like in one week and on one team.
